Threats to Computer System

Security threats are not only names to memorise. For this lesson, the key skill is to analyse how a threat causes harm: identify the threat, explain the immediate effect, and connect it to wider operational, financial, privacy or ethical consequences.

Conceptual Anchor

The Castle Defense Analogy

A computer system is like a castle containing valuable information. Malware is like something harmful that gets inside and damages or steals assets. Physical risks are threats to the building itself — fire, flood, theft, power problems or overheating. Cracking is entering restricted areas or bypassing security without permission. The important exam question is not only “What is the threat?” but also “What problems can it cause?”

Threat → Effect → Consequence

For an analyse question, build a chain of reasoning. Do not stop after naming the threat.

StageQuestion to askExample: ransomware
1. ThreatWhat happened?Ransomware infects a school server.
2. Immediate effectWhat does it directly do?Files are encrypted and cannot be accessed.
3. Operational consequenceHow does work or service change?Staff cannot access records or systems.
4. Wider consequenceWhy does that matter?Lessons and administration may be disrupted; recovery may cost money and damage trust.

Exam technique

“Ransomware encrypts files” is usually only the start. A stronger answer continues: encrypted files → unavailable data → disrupted service → financial / safety / reputational impact.

Malware

Malware is malicious software designed to perform an unauthorised or harmful action on a system. Different types spread or behave differently, but their consequences can overlap.

TypeHow it behavesPossible problems
VirusAttaches to files or system areas and spreads when infected content is executed or shared.Corrupted or deleted files, reduced performance, disruption.
WormSelf-replicates and spreads between systems or across networks without needing to attach to a host file.Network congestion, rapid infection of many devices, service disruption.
TrojanDisguises itself as legitimate software or a normal file to trick a user into running it.Unauthorised access, stolen data, installation of further malware.
RansomwareRestricts access to systems or encrypts data and demands payment.Loss of availability, downtime, recovery cost, possible data loss or disclosure.
SpywareSecretly monitors activity or collects information.Loss of privacy, stolen credentials, identity or financial harm.

Physical Risks

Physical risks threaten hardware, storage media, infrastructure or the environment in which a computer system operates. Their effects often lead to loss of availability, loss of data, equipment damage and recovery cost.

Physical riskImmediate effectWider problems
FireHardware and storage devices may be destroyed.Data loss, long downtime, replacement cost.
Flood / water leakServers, power systems or storage may be damaged.Systems unavailable, possible permanent data loss.
TheftDevices or storage media are removed.Loss of service plus possible disclosure of confidential data.
Power failureSystems stop unexpectedly.Unsaved work may be lost; services become unavailable.
Power surgeElectrical components may be damaged.Hardware failure, downtime and replacement cost.
OverheatingComponents become unstable or fail.Reduced reliability, shutdowns and hardware damage.
Accidental damage / vandalismEquipment is physically damaged.Interrupted service, repair cost, possible data loss.
Natural disasterLarge-scale damage to buildings and infrastructure.Extended downtime and difficult recovery.

Threat vs consequence

Flood is the threat. Damaged servers, unavailable services and lost data are consequences. Keep these roles separate in exam answers.

Computer System Cracking & Ethics

Meaning in this lesson

Here, computer system cracking means gaining unauthorised access to a system, data or resource, or bypassing its security without permission. The focus is on the ethical problems and consequences, not on attack techniques.

Ethical issueWhy it is a problemPossible consequence
Privacy / confidentialityPeople's data is accessed without consent or authorisation.Exposure of personal, medical, academic or financial information.
IntegrityData may be changed, deleted or falsified without permission.Incorrect records, unsafe decisions, loss of trust.
AvailabilitySystems may be disrupted or made unusable.Users cannot access services when needed.
Financial harmVictims may face theft, recovery cost or lost business.Direct financial loss and operational disruption.
Trust and responsibilityUnauthorised access abuses trust and ignores rules or consent.Reputational damage and reduced confidence in the organisation.
Intellectual propertyPrivate designs, source code or research may be copied without permission.Loss of competitive advantage or ownership rights.

1 Permission changes the ethical context

A security professional who has explicit authorisation to test a system and stays within the agreed scope is different from a person who bypasses security without permission. Ethical analysis should consider authorisation, consent, purpose, scope and harm.

Common Pitfalls

Virus vs Worm

A virus normally attaches to files or system areas and spreads when infected content is executed or shared. A worm is designed to self-replicate and spread independently, often across networks.

Malware ≠ Virus

Malware is the broad category. Virus, worm, Trojan, ransomware and spyware are examples of malware.

Threat ≠ Consequence

“Fire” is a threat. “Hardware destroyed, data unavailable and services interrupted” describes consequences.

Cracking ≠ Authorised Testing

Permission matters. Testing with explicit authorisation and agreed scope is ethically different from bypassing security without permission.

Exam Practice

Q1: A hospital's computer network is infected with ransomware. Staff cannot access patient records for several hours. Analyse two problems that may arise. [4]

Reveal marking points
1 mark for each valid problem + 1 mark for linked consequence, for example: • Patient records are unavailable → doctors may lack information needed for treatment. • Hospital systems are disrupted → appointments/treatment may be delayed, affecting service or patient safety.

Q2: A server room is flooded overnight. Analyse two problems this may cause for the organisation. [4]

Reveal marking points
Examples: • Servers/storage may be physically damaged → data or services may become unavailable. • Equipment may need replacement → recovery creates financial cost and extended downtime.

Q3: A student gains unauthorised access to the school database and reads confidential records but does not modify them. Analyse two ethical problems. [4]

Reveal marking points
Examples: • Privacy/confidentiality is violated because records are viewed without permission → students' personal information is exposed. • Trust and authorisation are breached → the school and students may lose confidence in the system even though the data was not changed.

Q4: Explain why authorised penetration/security testing is ethically different from unauthorised cracking. [3]

Reveal marking points
Authorised testing has explicit permission and an agreed purpose/scope. The tester is expected to report weaknesses rather than exploit them for unauthorised gain or harm. Unauthorised cracking bypasses security without permission.
