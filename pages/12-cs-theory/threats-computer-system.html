Threats to Computer System

Security questions are not only about naming a threat. A strong answer explains how the threat works, what it directly affects and what wider problem follows. This page brings together the threat types and comparisons used in class.

Conceptual Anchor

The Castle Defense Analogy

A computer system is like a castle containing valuable information. Malware is like something harmful that gets inside and damages or steals assets. Physical risks are threats to the building itself — fire, flood, theft, power problems or overheating. Cracking is entering restricted areas or bypassing security without permission. The important exam question is not only “What is the threat?” but also “What problems can it cause?”

Threat → Effect → Consequence

For an analyse question, build a chain of reasoning. Do not stop after naming the threat.

StageQuestion to askExample: ransomware
1. ThreatWhat happened?Ransomware infects a school server.
2. Immediate effectWhat does it directly do?Files are encrypted and cannot be accessed.
3. Operational consequenceHow does work or service change?Staff cannot access records or systems.
4. Wider consequenceWhy does that matter?Lessons and administration may be disrupted; recovery may cost money and damage trust.

Exam technique

“Ransomware encrypts files” is usually only the start. A stronger answer continues: encrypted files → unavailable data → disrupted service → financial / safety / reputational impact.

Malware

Malware means malicious software. It is software designed to harm or disrupt a system, steal information or gain unauthorised access. A single incident may involve more than one malware type.

Virus, Worm & Trojan Horse

TypeDistinguishing featureHow it spreads / worksExample consequence
VirusAttaches to a host file or programReplicates when infected code executesCorrupts or deletes files; disrupts work
WormStandalone and self-replicatingCan spread automatically between systems, often across networksRapid infection, bandwidth use, service disruption
Trojan horseDisguises malicious code as legitimate softwareUser is persuaded to run it; it does not self-replicateBackdoor access, credential theft, further malware

Ransomware, Spyware & Scareware

TypePrimary behaviourVisibilityMain impact
RansomwareDenies access to files or systems, commonly through encryption, and demands paymentUsually obvious after activationLoss of availability, downtime, recovery cost; sometimes data disclosure
SpywareSecretly monitors activity and collects informationDesigned to remain hiddenPrivacy loss, credential theft, surveillance
ScarewareUses false security warnings to frighten users into paying or installing softwareHighly visible and deliberately alarmingFinancial fraud, panic, installation of unwanted or malicious software
Real-world case studies

WannaCry: ransomware that spread rapidly across vulnerable Windows systems and disrupted organisations around the world.

Pegasus: spyware used for covert surveillance, including access to sensitive device information and communications.

These examples are useful because they contrast availability-focused harm with confidentiality/privacy-focused harm.

Other Cyber Attacks

DoS & DDoS

A Denial of Service (DoS) attack makes a service unavailable to legitimate users by exhausting resources or exploiting a weakness. A DDoS attack does this using many distributed sources, often compromised devices in a botnet.

1 DDoS cause-and-effect chain

Many compromised devicesOften controlled as a botnet
Flood the targetTraffic / requests consume finite resources
Legitimate users blockedService becomes slow or unavailable

Important

A DoS attack does not require the server to crash. The key problem is that legitimate requests cannot be processed normally.

Phishing vs Pharming

FeaturePhishingPharming
Initial techniqueDeceptive email, text, call or website impersonates a trusted sourceDNS, hosts file, router or similar redirection is manipulated
Route to fake siteUsually follows a deceptive link or messageMay type the correct address and still be redirected
Main targetHuman trust and sensitive informationPeople and systems through technical redirection
Possible impactCredential theft, account takeover, malware deliveryCredential theft, financial loss, large-scale redirection

2 Two routes to the same fake login page

Phishing

Fake IT email → user clicks link → fake login page → credentials captured.

Pharming

User types correct address → manipulated DNS/host resolution → fake login page → credentials captured.

Social Engineering

Social engineering manipulates people into revealing information or performing an unsafe action. Common tactics include impersonation, urgency, fear and offers of a reward.

Identify the attack from the mechanism.

Online Tracking

Online tracking collects and links information about a user's activity across visits or websites. Tracking is not automatically malware, but it can create privacy risks.

MethodHow it supports tracking
Tracking cookieStores an identifier that can link browsing activity across visits; some third-party cookies can link activity across different sites.
Tracking pixelAn embedded resource sends a request to a server when content loads, allowing a visit or action to be recorded.
Device fingerprintingCombines browser and device characteristics to distinguish or recognise a device, sometimes without cookies.

3 Tracking risk chain

Activity collectedPages, searches, clicks, device details
Data linkedSeveral visits or sites are connected
Profile createdInterests, routines or identity may be inferred

Possible privacy problems

Detailed profiling, loss of anonymity, unwanted targeting and sharing linked information with third parties.

Identify the tracking mechanism.

Physical Risks

Physical risks threaten hardware, storage media, infrastructure or the environment in which a computer system operates. Their effects often lead to loss of availability, loss of data, equipment damage and recovery cost.

Physical riskImmediate effectWider problems
FireHardware and storage devices may be destroyed.Data loss, long downtime, replacement cost.
Flood / water leakServers, power systems or storage may be damaged.Systems unavailable, possible permanent data loss.
TheftDevices or storage media are removed.Loss of service plus possible disclosure of confidential data.
Power failureSystems stop unexpectedly.Unsaved work may be lost; services become unavailable.
Power surgeElectrical components may be damaged.Hardware failure, downtime and replacement cost.
OverheatingComponents become unstable or fail.Reduced reliability, shutdowns and hardware damage.
Accidental damage / vandalismEquipment is physically damaged.Interrupted service, repair cost, possible data loss.
Natural disasterLarge-scale damage to buildings and infrastructure.Extended downtime and difficult recovery.

Threat vs consequence

Flood is the threat. Damaged servers, unavailable services and lost data are consequences. Keep these roles separate in exam answers.

Computer System Cracking & Ethics

Meaning in this lesson

Here, computer system cracking means gaining unauthorised access to a system, data or resource, or bypassing its security without permission. The focus is on the ethical problems and consequences, not on attack techniques.

1 Permission and intent

TypePermissionTypical behaviour
White hatExplicit permissionSecurity testing within an agreed scope
Black hatNo permissionMalicious access for theft, disruption, fraud or other harm
Grey hatOften no permissionMay investigate or disclose a weakness without malicious intent, but access is still unauthorised

Good intention ≠ authorisation

Reporting a weakness does not automatically make the original testing authorised. Permission and agreed scope remain important.

Ethical issueWhy it is a problemPossible consequence
Privacy / confidentialityPeople's data is accessed without consent or authorisation.Exposure of personal, medical, academic or financial information.
IntegrityData may be changed, deleted or falsified without permission.Incorrect records, unsafe decisions, loss of trust.
AvailabilitySystems may be disrupted or made unusable.Users cannot access services when needed.
Financial harmVictims may face theft, recovery cost or lost business.Direct financial loss and operational disruption.
Trust and responsibilityUnauthorised access abuses trust and ignores rules or consent.Reputational damage and reduced confidence in the organisation.
Intellectual propertyPrivate designs, source code or research may be copied without permission.Loss of competitive advantage or ownership rights.

1 Permission changes the ethical context

A security professional who has explicit authorisation to test a system and stays within the agreed scope is different from a person who bypasses security without permission. Ethical analysis should consider authorisation, consent, purpose, scope and harm.

Additional Threats — Extension

Rootkit, keylogger, adware, password attacks & data interception
Threat / techniqueConcise meaning
RootkitSoftware designed to conceal malicious activity and maintain privileged access.
KeyloggerRecords keystrokes and may capture usernames, passwords or messages; malicious keylogging is a form of spyware.
AdwareDisplays advertisements; some adware also tracks users or changes browser settings. Advertising software is not automatically malicious.
Brute forceSystematically tries many possible passwords.
Dictionary attackTries likely passwords from lists of common words or previously exposed passwords.
Credential stuffingTries username/password pairs stolen from another service.
Data interception / MitMAn attacker captures or relays data travelling between devices; readable traffic may expose private messages or login details.

Common Pitfalls

Virus vs Worm

A virus normally attaches to files or system areas and spreads when infected content is executed or shared. A worm is designed to self-replicate and spread independently, often across networks.

Malware ≠ Virus

Malware is the broad category. Virus, worm, Trojan, ransomware and spyware are examples of malware.

Threat ≠ Consequence

“Fire” is a threat. “Hardware destroyed, data unavailable and services interrupted” describes consequences.

Cracking ≠ Authorised Testing

Permission matters. Testing with explicit authorisation and agreed scope is ethically different from bypassing security without permission.

Phishing ≠ pharming

Phishing commonly relies on a deceptive message/link. Pharming can redirect a correctly typed web address.

DoS ≠ DDoS

Both target availability, but DDoS uses distributed sources against the same target.

Tracking ≠ automatically malware

Tracking can be used for analytics or advertising, but it can still create privacy risks.

Exam Practice

Q1: Explain two differences between a virus and a worm. [4]

Reveal marking points
Virus needs a host file/program whereas a worm is standalone. [2] Virus replication depends on infected code executing, whereas a worm can self-replicate/spread independently across systems or networks. [2]

Q2: A teacher receives an email linking to a fake school login page. Explain how this attack could compromise confidential data. [4]

Reveal marking points
Deceptive email impersonates a trusted sender [1]. Teacher follows the link / reaches fake login page [1]. Credentials are entered and captured [1]. Attacker uses them to access confidential school records [1].

Q3: Distinguish a DoS attack from a DDoS attack. [2]

Reveal marking points
Both aim to deny legitimate access / exhaust resources [1]. DDoS uses many distributed sources, often compromised devices in a botnet [1].

Q4: A browser extension secretly records a teacher's keystrokes and sends them to another person. Identify the specific threat and explain what it could capture. [2]

Reveal marking points
Keylogger / spyware that records keystrokes [1]. Could capture passwords, usernames, messages or other typed information [1].
